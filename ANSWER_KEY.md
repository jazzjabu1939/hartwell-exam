## ALPHA — NIGERIA

### Q1

**Stem:** Hartwell's market entry analysis of Nigeria highlights capital controls imposed by the Central Bank, restrictions on foreign exchange access, and a history of limits on profit repatriation. In international business terms, this is primarily an example of:

- **A:** Ownership risk — the threat that the government may nationalize or expropriate Hartwell's local assets without fair or timely compensation, permanently eliminating Hartwell's equity stake in the operation and foreclosing any future claim to the asset's value
- **B:** ✅ **CORRECT** Transfer risk — the risk that profits and capital cannot be freely moved out of the country
- **C:** Operational risk — the risk that political instability, social unrest, or deteriorating infrastructure will disrupt Hartwell's day-to-day business activities in ways that prevent the company from fulfilling orders, managing staff, or maintaining normal commercial operations
- **D:** Reputational risk — the risk that Hartwell's brand equity and consumer trust will be damaged by association with a politically unstable or internationally stigmatized country, making it harder to position the brand as a premium product globally

**Correct:** B

---

### Q2

**Stem:** Hartwell prices its blenders in US dollars and sells through a Nigerian distributor who pays in naira. Over the past two years, the naira has lost approximately 50% of its value against the dollar. What is the most direct operational consequence of this currency movement for Hartwell's business?

- **A:** Hartwell's products become cheaper for Nigerian consumers relative to locally priced alternatives, which might increase market demand and drive volume growth — though this ignores that the distributor must still purchase those products at Hartwell's unchanged dollar prices, which have become far more expensive in local currency terms
- **B:** ✅ **CORRECT** Hartwell's dollar-denominated revenues decline because the naira depreciation makes it economically impossible for the distributor to maintain purchase volumes at dollar-denominated prices
- **C:** Hartwell is fully insulated because pricing is set in USD and the distributor contractually absorbs all currency exposure — though in practice a distributor that can no longer afford to purchase dollar-priced goods will simply stop ordering, making Hartwell's contractual insulation commercially meaningless if the distributor cannot sustain its purchasing power
- **D:** Hartwell benefits because the weaker naira makes Nigerian production cheaper, reducing Hartwell's manufacturing costs — though Hartwell manufactures in the US, not Nigeria, so Nigerian currency movements have no bearing on Hartwell's production cost structure whatsoever

**Correct:** B

---

### Q3

**Stem:** Feedback from Hartwell's Nigerian distribution partner reveals that local employees feel uncomfortable raising concerns directly with senior expatriate managers, preferring to communicate through intermediaries or in informal one-on-one settings rather than in group meetings. This behavior pattern is most consistent with which Hofstede cultural dimension?

- **A:** High Individualism — individualist cultures are characterized by self-reliance and personal initiative, and employees in high-individualism contexts typically prefer to manage workplace conflicts through direct one-on-one negotiation rather than collective group processes — however, this misattributes a collectivist group-harmony behavior pattern to an individualist framework
- **B:** ✅ **CORRECT** High Power Distance — employees accept hierarchical authority and are reluctant to challenge or question those in senior positions, especially in public settings
- **C:** High Uncertainty Avoidance — in high-UA cultures employees prefer structured environments and clear rules, and they may avoid ambiguous situations where the outcome of speaking up to authority is uncertain; however, the behavioral preference for indirect communication through intermediaries rather than direct group challenge is more precisely explained by hierarchical deference than by uncertainty about outcomes
- **D:** High Masculinity — in highly masculine cultures, showing vulnerability or emotional openness in professional settings can undermine one's status in a competitive, achievement-oriented environment; however, the preference for intermediaries and informal one-on-one channels over group discussion reflects hierarchical deference more directly than competitive self-presentation concerns

**Correct:** B

---

### Q4

**Stem:** Hartwell's legal team is reviewing the trade environment before finalizing its import and market strategy for Nigeria. Which of the following statements about Nigeria's trade and regulatory context is most accurate and most relevant to Hartwell's planning?

- **A:** ✅ **CORRECT** Nigeria is a member of ECOWAS, which means goods produced in other West African member states can enter Nigeria with preferential tariff treatment, potentially exposing Hartwell to competition from ECOWAS-based manufacturers
- **B:** Nigeria's WTO membership sets binding commitments on tariff schedules and Most-Favoured-Nation treatment, but it does not cap consumer goods tariffs at 5% — Nigeria maintains tariffs well above that threshold on many consumer product categories, and WTO membership establishes floors and ceilings negotiated per product category, not a universal low-tariff guarantee
- **C:** Nigeria's local content requirements originated in the oil and gas sector but have been expanding in scope and are increasingly applied to consumer goods, manufacturing, and services sectors as part of Nigeria's broader industrial policy agenda — treating them as exclusively applicable to oil and gas significantly underestimates the regulatory risk for a consumer goods importer
- **D:** ECOWAS does provide preferential tariff arrangements for goods traded among member states, but these benefits are conditional on meeting rules-of-origin requirements — goods must contain sufficient local value-added content to qualify, and goods produced outside the ECOWAS region and merely transshipped cannot access the preferential rates

**Correct:** A

### Q5

**Stem:** Given the characteristics of the Nigerian market as described — significant currency and political risk, infrastructure gaps outside major cities, an opaque regulatory environment, and the importance of local knowledge and relationships — which entry mode is most appropriate for Hartwell's initial entry, and why?

- **A:** Wholly Owned Subsidiary (WOS) — provides Hartwell maximum control over operations, brand standards, and profits without sharing revenue with a partner, but requires substantial upfront capital, exposes Hartwell to full financial risk in a high-volatility market before it has learned local operating conditions, and requires navigating an opaque regulatory environment without the local knowledge needed to do so effectively
- **B:** Joint Venture with a Nigerian partner — combines Hartwell's products and brand with a partner's local knowledge, regulatory relationships, and distribution network, though it requires finding a trustworthy partner, negotiating complex governance and profit-sharing arrangements, and investing capital before Hartwell fully understands the market dynamics it is entering
- **C:** ✅ **CORRECT** Export via a local distributor — minimizes capital at risk, leverages existing local expertise, and allows Hartwell to learn the market with limited downside before committing deeper resources
- **D:** Licensing to a local manufacturer — allows Hartwell to earn royalty income without operational exposure in a high-risk market, but it surrenders brand quality control, creates significant IP exposure by sharing product designs with a local entity, and provides no market learning that would be needed if Hartwell later decides to deepen its commitment

**Correct:** C

## ALPHA — CHINA

### Q1

**Stem:** Hartwell's competitive advantage is built on proprietary motor design and engineering technology. Before entering China, Hartwell's General Counsel identifies IP protection as a top-tier risk. Which of the following best describes the IP protection challenge in China for a company like Hartwell?

- **A:** China does not have a functioning patent system, so foreign companies cannot register intellectual property and are fully exposed to unauthorized copying — this overstates the problem; China has a well-developed patent registration system administered by CNIPA with millions of active registrations, though the real challenge is inconsistent enforcement and slow judicial resolution, not the absence of a registration mechanism
- **B:** ✅ **CORRECT** China's IP laws have improved significantly in recent years, but enforcement remains inconsistent, and Hartwell's designs could be copied before a legal dispute is resolved — particularly if the competitor is a well-connected domestic firm
- **C:** Hartwell's products are automatically protected in China by international patent law once they are commercially sold there, without requiring separate in-country registration — this is incorrect; international patent exhaustion doctrine does not create automatic cross-border protection, and China requires separate CNIPA registration; even registered patents face enforcement challenges that make active protection essential
- **D:** The primary IP risk in China is trademark squatting — registering foreign brand names before the foreign company arrives — rather than product copying, so Hartwell's engineering designs face limited exposure; however, for a company whose competitive advantage is proprietary motor technology, product design copying is a far more acute strategic threat than brand name registration

**Correct:** B

---

### Q2

**Stem:** Hartwell's legal counsel notes that China's consumer appliance sector does not formally require a Joint Venture — Hartwell could legally enter as a Wholly Foreign-Owned Enterprise (WFOE). The counsel nonetheless strongly recommends proactive investment in government relations at the local and provincial levels. What is the most compelling reason for this advice?

- **A:** Chinese law requires all foreign companies, including WFOEs, to appoint a government-approved director to their local board of directors — while WFOE structures do require certain corporate governance disclosures and registration of directors, there is no universal legal requirement to appoint a government-designated director; this overstates formal legal obligations and conflates regulatory compliance with the informal relationship dynamics counsel is actually addressing
- **B:** ✅ **CORRECT** Regulatory approvals, business license renewals, enforcement discretion, and local policy implementation in China are significantly influenced by relationships with relevant government officials — making government relations a practical operational necessity, not just a courtesy
- **C:** Without formal government relations, Hartwell would be legally ineligible to sell through Chinese e-commerce platforms, where the majority of premium appliance sales occur — e-commerce platforms like Tmall and JD.com do have seller qualification requirements, but these relate to product certification and business registration, not formal government relationship investment; platform eligibility is a regulatory compliance matter, not a relationship management outcome
- **D:** A WFOE structure in China requires annual government review, and companies without established relationships routinely fail these reviews — WFOEs do face annual compliance filings and standard regulatory reporting, but these are administrative requirements; failure rates are not meaningfully correlated with government relationship investment, and conflating annual filings with discretionary enforcement mischaracterizes how the regulatory system operates

**Correct:** B

### Q3

**Stem:** Hartwell's China country manager is negotiating a distribution agreement with a regional partner in Chengdu. After three meetings, the partner has not raised price or contract terms — instead inviting the country manager to dinners, a golf outing, and a factory tour. The country manager is frustrated. What cultural concept best explains the partner's behavior?

- **A:** ✅ **CORRECT** The partner is exhibiting high long-term orientation and guanxi-building behavior — in Chinese business culture, investing in personal trust and relationship quality before formalizing terms is not a delay; it is the negotiation
- **B:** The partner has high uncertainty avoidance and is stalling because Chinese business culture is deeply risk-averse about formal commitments with foreign companies — while uncertainty avoidance does shape Chinese business behavior, high UA typically manifests as requests for detailed written contracts and formal risk disclosures upfront, not extended social hospitality; the pattern of dinners, golf, and factory tours points to relationship investment, not risk mitigation behavior
- **C:** The partner is operating from high individualism — conducting independent due diligence and will make a unilateral decision without involving the country manager in his internal process — China scores relatively low on individualism; the collective hospitality approach of organizing group outings and a factory tour is the opposite of individualist behavior and reflects group-oriented relationship investment, not independent personal decision-making
- **D:** The partner is signaling low power distance — he wants to establish peer equality before negotiating, reflecting a preference for flat, non-hierarchical business relationships — low power distance cultures tend to reduce formality and emphasize directness; elaborate hospitality rituals and structured factory tours reflect a high-context, relationship-oriented culture, not an effort to flatten hierarchy between negotiating parties

**Correct:** A

### Q4

**Stem:** New US tariffs on foreign-made goods have given Hartwell breathing room domestically. Hartwell is now considering whether a manufacturing presence in China could help it sidestep tariff exposure on imported components. What is the most significant strategic risk of this plan in the current US-China trade environment?

- **A:** Manufacturing in China would make Hartwell's products eligible for Chinese export subsidies, which would violate WTO rules and expose Hartwell to trade sanctions — while export subsidies do exist in China, receiving them does not automatically constitute a WTO violation for the recipient company, and the more immediately significant risk for Hartwell is not subsidy eligibility but the IP and technology exposure that arises during any manufacturing partnership arrangement in the current geopolitical environment
- **B:** ✅ **CORRECT** Establishing manufacturing or a JV presence in China could create technology transfer pressure — formal or informal requirements that Hartwell share proprietary designs and engineering with Chinese partners or government entities
- **C:** China's manufacturing sector is operating at full capacity, meaning Hartwell would face multi-year delays securing factory space and qualified production workers — while capacity constraints are a real operational consideration in some sectors, China has significant and broadly available manufacturing capacity, particularly in consumer electronics and appliances; capacity is not the primary strategic barrier for a company considering China manufacturing
- **D:** US tariffs apply to all revenue generated by US-headquartered companies globally, regardless of where goods are manufactured, so relocating production to China would provide no tariff benefit — US import tariffs are applied based on country of origin of the physical goods, not on the global revenue of US-headquartered companies; a China-manufactured product shipped to a third market would not automatically carry a US tariff burden

**Correct:** B

### Q5

**Stem:** Hartwell is choosing between three entry modes for China: (1) a Wholly Foreign-Owned Enterprise (WFOE), (2) a 50/50 Joint Venture with a Chinese appliance distributor, and (3) a licensing agreement with a Chinese manufacturer. Given Hartwell's priority of protecting its proprietary technology and maintaining brand quality standards, which mode is most consistent with those goals?

- **A:** Licensing — it generates royalty income with minimal capital commitment, and quality can be controlled through the licensing contract terms — however, licensing is the entry mode with the weakest IP protection available; the licensee gains access to Hartwell's proprietary technology as a fundamental condition of the arrangement, creating the very IP exposure Hartwell is prioritizing to avoid
- **B:** ✅ **CORRECT** WFOE — it gives Hartwell full operational control, IP protection, and brand quality oversight, but requires greater capital investment and management capacity to establish and run
- **C:** 50/50 Joint Venture — it divides governance equally between Hartwell and a local partner, potentially offering local knowledge; however, shared governance means Hartwell cannot unilaterally enforce brand standards, protect IP, or exit quickly if the partner misuses technology — equal voting rights create strategic deadlock risk on precisely the decisions where unilateral control matters most
- **D:** WFOE — it eliminates all interaction with Chinese regulatory authorities, allowing Hartwell to operate in China the same way it operates in the United States — this is incorrect; a WFOE does provide operational control but does not eliminate the need to comply with Chinese corporate law, annual filings, sector-specific licensing, tax regulation, and ongoing engagement with regulatory authorities that all foreign businesses must maintain

**Correct:** B

## BETA — EXPAND

### Q1

**Stem:** After three years, Hartwell has established brand recognition, a working distribution network, and profitability in its international market. The board approves a plan to push the existing blender and coffee maker lineup into 12 additional cities and expand the number of retail partners in existing cities. In the Ansoff Matrix, this growth strategy is best described as:

- **A:** Market Development — selling existing products to new customer segments or new geographic markets that the company has not previously served; however, the Ansoff framework distinguishes expansion within existing market types from entry into genuinely new ones — Hartwell is intensifying coverage in markets where it already operates, not reaching previously unserved segments or geographies
- **B:** Product Development — introducing new products to the existing customer base in established markets to grow share among buyers who already know the brand; Hartwell is not changing its product offering at all, and this strategy would require R&D investment in new SKUs, which is not part of the plan
- **C:** ✅ **CORRECT** Market Penetration — selling existing products in existing markets with greater intensity, coverage, and resource investment
- **D:** Diversification — expanding into new markets with new products simultaneously, which represents the highest-risk Ansoff quadrant because the company faces uncertainty on both the product and market dimensions at once; Hartwell is doing neither, staying entirely within its proven product lineup and established market base

**Correct:** C

### Q2

**Stem:** Hartwell's CEO argues that the company's core competitive asset — premium engineering quality — is universally valued. She proposes a fully standardized global product lineup manufactured at a single production hub, distributed worldwide with consistent pricing and brand positioning. In the Bartlett & Ghoshal framework, this strategic posture is best described as:

- **A:** Multidomestic — a multidomestic strategy would involve adapting product specifications, pricing, marketing, and potentially manufacturing to the distinct requirements of individual national markets; the CEO's proposal is the structural opposite, mandating a single global standard with no local variation permitted
- **B:** Transnational — a transnational strategy explicitly balances global integration with local responsiveness, typically involving some degree of product or marketing adaptation for local markets; the CEO's proposal eliminates local adaptation entirely in favor of pure standardization, which is incompatible with the transnational model's defining characteristic
- **C:** ✅ **CORRECT** Global — treating the world as a single integrated market, pursuing cost and brand advantages through standardization and scale
- **D:** International — an international strategy exports existing home-country products with minimal modification, relying on the brand's home-market reputation, while allowing subsidiaries to make modest adaptations within a headquarters-defined framework; the CEO's proposal is more rigidly standardized than international strategy, which preserves at least some local discretion

**Correct:** C

### Q3

**Stem:** Hartwell's current supply chain relies on air freight from the US to a single in-country warehouse. The VP of Operations warns this model cannot scale cost-effectively to 12 additional cities. Which adjustment best addresses both cost and service level as Hartwell's footprint expands?

- **A:** ✅ **CORRECT** Switch to ocean freight for bulk shipments and establish multiple regional distribution points in-country, positioning inventory closer to demand clusters and reducing per-unit logistics cost
- **B:** Double air freight capacity by adding a second routing, while keeping the central warehouse as the single fulfillment node — this increases per-unit logistics cost rather than reducing it, and a single central warehouse means all goods must travel longer last-mile distances to reach 12 new cities, worsening service levels for markets far from the central node rather than solving the underlying infrastructure problem
- **C:** Outsource all in-country logistics entirely to the distribution partner and remove Hartwell's operations team from the supply chain — full outsourcing to the distributor eliminates Hartwell's visibility into inventory positioning, delivery standards, and stockout risk, making it harder to hold the partner accountable for performance and creating service reliability problems as the geographic footprint expands
- **D:** Reduce the product catalog to the single highest-margin SKU to simplify logistics requirements before expanding geographically — this sacrifices the product breadth that justifies Hartwell's premium shelf space and retailer relationships, addressing logistics complexity by eliminating revenue opportunities rather than solving the actual infrastructure challenge

**Correct:** A

### Q4

**Stem:** As Hartwell expands to 12 new cities, the marketing team debates whether to maintain the established 'American premium' brand positioning or develop locally resonant messaging. What principle of international brand management most strongly supports maintaining the consistent positioning?

- **A:** Brands must always adapt messaging to local cultural references in each new market to maximize consumer relevance and sales conversion — while some localization can improve resonance, a premium brand built on aspirational positioning risks diluting its premium equity by fragmenting the brand story across markets; consumers in secondary cities often value the same national premium positioning that drove adoption in major cities
- **B:** ✅ **CORRECT** A consistent premium brand identity creates a 'halo effect' — recognition and aspirational value earned in the major cities transfer credibly to new markets, where early adopters want the same product that established consumers in larger cities use
- **C:** Premium positioning is only effective in Tier 1 cities; Hartwell must adapt to a mid-market positioning for secondary cities to remain price-competitive — this mid-market repositioning would undercut the brand equity Hartwell has built and create price-channel conflict with premium retailers in Tier 1 cities who would resist a brand positioning itself differently in nearby secondary markets
- **D:** The American origin of Hartwell's products is a liability in most international consumer markets and should be deemphasized in all marketing materials — research on premium consumer goods in most international markets shows that country-of-origin effects are often a positive signal; American origin in appliances is frequently associated with quality and innovation, making it a brand asset rather than a liability

**Correct:** B

### Q5

**Stem:** Hartwell has been operating as an exporter with a local distribution partner. Following the market penetration strategy's success, the CFO proposes reinvesting a portion of local profits to lease a local warehouse and hire a small in-country operations team. In FDI theory, this move is best understood as:

- **A:** Greenfield FDI — Hartwell is building new productive capacity in the foreign market from scratch rather than acquiring existing assets — greenfield FDI specifically involves constructing entirely new productive facilities, such as factories or offices, built from the ground up; leasing existing warehouse space and hiring a small local operations team is an incremental commercial investment, not new-capacity construction in the greenfield sense
- **B:** Horizontal FDI — Hartwell is replicating its home-country business operations abroad in order to serve the local market directly — horizontal FDI describes replicating the same core business function in a foreign market, such as building a local production plant; leasing a warehouse and hiring operations staff is a logistical investment to support distribution, not a full replication of Hartwell's manufacturing and commercial operations
- **C:** ✅ **CORRECT** FDI deepening — Hartwell is increasing its operational commitment and asset base in an existing market, moving from arm's-length export to a more embedded and controlled presence
- **D:** Vertical FDI — Hartwell is backward-integrating into its supply chain by establishing local manufacturing capacity — vertical FDI involves integrating into a different stage of the value chain, such as acquiring a supplier or moving production; Hartwell is not acquiring suppliers or becoming a local manufacturer; it is deepening its commercial and logistical presence in a distribution role

**Correct:** C

## BETA — DIVERSIFY

### Q1

**Stem:** Hartwell's current product line — premium blenders and espresso machines — was designed for the American 'prosumer' market. The diversification strategy calls for launching rice cookers, multi-cookers, and wok accessories adapted to local cooking practices. In global strategy terms, this decision represents a shift toward which strategic posture?

- **A:** Global strategy — a global strategy would standardize the product portfolio across all markets, selling identical blenders and coffee makers worldwide without modification to achieve scale; launching locally adapted rice cookers and multi-cookers specifically for this market's cooking practices is fundamentally incompatible with the standardization logic that defines global strategy
- **B:** ✅ **CORRECT** Multidomestic strategy — designing products specifically for local consumer needs, accepting that what succeeds in this market will differ from what succeeds in the US
- **C:** Transnational strategy — a transnational approach maintains shared global engineering platforms while adapting specific features for local markets, for example using the same motor platform across product lines but varying capacity and settings by region; Hartwell is creating entirely new product categories for this market rather than adapting a shared platform, which goes beyond transnational adaptation
- **D:** International strategy — an international strategy exports the existing US lineup with minimal modification, relying on the home-country brand's premium reputation to generate demand in foreign markets; the entire premise of the diversification decision is that the US lineup is not suited to local cooking practices, which directly contradicts the assumption that home-country products can be exported without adaptation

**Correct:** B

---

### Q2

**Stem:** In the Bartlett & Ghoshal framework, 'local responsiveness' is the degree to which a firm adapts its strategy to the specific requirements of individual national markets. Which factor in Hartwell's situation most strongly argues for a high local responsiveness approach in the product diversification decision?

- **A:** Hartwell's premium engineering reputation is a universal competitive advantage that transcends differences in local cooking culture — engineering quality is a genuine competitive asset, but it is a horizontal advantage applying equally across product categories rather than a market-specific advantage; premium quality in blenders designed for American kitchens does not override the functional mismatch between those products and local cooking requirements
- **B:** ✅ **CORRECT** Local competitors who design products specifically for local cuisine have a built-in and sustained advantage over a foreign brand selling appliances designed for American kitchens — an advantage that brand prestige alone cannot overcome
- **C:** Manufacturing products tailored to local markets creates cost disadvantages that will make Hartwell unprofitable against price-competitive local brands — while local product development does carry higher unit costs at lower initial volumes, the alternative of selling products designed for American kitchens that local consumers do not actually use generates zero revenue regardless of cost structure; the cost argument conflates input cost with revenue realization
- **D:** Consumer preferences for kitchen appliances are converging globally due to social media influence, reducing the need for product adaptation in most markets — social media has accelerated awareness of food trends and brand exposure across cultures, but it has not standardized cooking equipment needs; rice, wok-based stir-frying, and multi-pot cooking remain structurally different from the blending and espresso-making applications Hartwell's current products are designed to serve

**Correct:** B

---

### Q3

**Stem:** Hartwell's board approves the diversification strategy but asks for a candid risk assessment. The CFO notes that launching new product categories in an international market is inherently more complex than expanding the existing line. Which statement best captures the compounding nature of the risk?

- **A:** New product categories are subject to higher import tariffs than product categories already cleared through customs, increasing landed cost — tariff classification is based on HS product codes and product function, not on whether a company has previously imported goods in a different category; new product categories do not automatically attract higher duties, and the tariff structure for appliances is determined by product specification, not commercial history
- **B:** ✅ **CORRECT** Hartwell faces simultaneous product development risk — the new products may not meet local performance or quality expectations — and brand extension risk — consumers who trust Hartwell for blenders may not automatically transfer that trust to rice cookers or woks
- **C:** International supply chains for new product categories are structurally more expensive than for established product lines, making near-term profitability structurally unlikely — while supply chain costs for new categories may be higher initially due to lower volumes and new supplier relationships, these are not permanently fixed disadvantages; costs typically normalize as volumes scale, and the more fundamental question is whether the products will succeed in the market at all
- **D:** Local governments typically require separate regulatory certification for new product categories, a process that can delay market entry by two or more years — while product certification timelines do vary by country and product type, a two-year universal delay is not the standard; certification can often be completed within six to eighteen months, and the certification challenge, while real, is a manageable operational hurdle rather than the compounding strategic risk the CFO is asking about

**Correct:** B

### Q4

**Stem:** Hartwell's product development team is designing a new rice cooker for the local market. A consumer research firm recommends testing three features: (1) a 'quick cook' mode (15 minutes), (2) a 'keep warm' function (up to 12 hours), and (3) a minimal industrial aesthetic consistent with Hartwell's US design. Which cultural product preference insight is most likely to emerge from the research?

- **A:** Consumers will most value the minimal design, because premium foreign brands are prized precisely for their distinctively foreign aesthetic — while foreign aesthetic design does command a premium in some aspirational consumer segments, for a kitchen appliance used multiple times daily, functional performance and usage pattern fit consistently outweigh aesthetic novelty; a beautiful appliance that does not match how consumers actually cook will generate high return rates and poor word-of-mouth
- **B:** ✅ **CORRECT** The 'keep warm' function is likely the highest-priority feature, because it aligns with the actual usage pattern — rice is typically cooked once and kept ready for consumption throughout the day in households where rice anchors every meal
- **C:** The 'quick cook' mode will be the priority, because time efficiency is the single most valued attribute for appliance consumers in emerging markets — while time efficiency is broadly valued, the 15-minute quick-cook mode addresses a use case — cooking rice for a single immediate meal — that is less common in households where rice is prepared in larger batches for consumption throughout the day; the quick-cook feature addresses the wrong usage pattern
- **D:** All three features will be valued equally, because premium appliance consumers are functionally homogeneous across cultures — consumer preference research consistently shows significant cross-cultural variation in kitchen appliance priorities driven by dietary staples, cooking methods, household size, and meal timing patterns; premium willingness-to-pay does not imply uniform feature preferences across culturally distinct markets

**Correct:** B

### Q5

**Stem:** Hartwell has limited management bandwidth and capital. The diversification strategy requires investment in new product development, new manufacturing partnerships, new supply chain logistics, and new consumer marketing — all while the existing product line needs continued attention. Which framework is most useful for guiding Hartwell's resource allocation between these competing investment demands?

- **A:** ✅ **CORRECT** The BCG Growth-Share Matrix — categorize the existing blender/coffee maker line as a 'cash cow' generating reliable profits, and allocate a portion of those profits to fund the new product categories as 'question marks' with uncertain but potentially high returns
- **B:** Porter's Five Forces — analyze competitive intensity in each new product category to determine which faces the weakest competition and should receive the most resources — Porter's Five Forces is a competitive analysis tool designed to assess industry attractiveness and margin potential within a given market; it helps identify which product categories face less competitive pressure but does not provide a mechanism for allocating capital and management bandwidth between competing internal investment demands
- **C:** The Ansoff Matrix — recognize that diversification is the highest-risk Ansoff quadrant and use the matrix to categorize the strategic logic of the decision; however, the Ansoff Matrix is a strategy classification tool, not a resource allocation framework — it helps describe the nature of the growth strategy but does not provide a mechanism for prioritizing investment between an existing cash-generating product line and new uncertain product categories
- **D:** Hofstede's cultural dimensions — predict which product categories will achieve the highest acceptance in the local culture before allocating product development resources — Hofstede's framework is valuable for understanding consumer preferences and management practices, but it is a cultural analysis tool, not a capital allocation framework; predicting category acceptance is an input to product development decisions, not a substitute for financial portfolio analysis of resource tradeoffs

**Correct:** A

## GAMMA — HR

### Q1

**Stem:** Hartwell has lost three regional sales managers in four months. Exit interviews consistently describe the same problems: local employees feel 'micromanaged,' 'undervalued,' and 'passed over for decisions about their own markets.' The expat leadership team from Columbus is surprised — they describe their management approach as 'hands-on and collaborative.' Which explanation best accounts for this disconnect?

- **A:** The local employees are underperforming and are using cultural complaints to deflect legitimate performance feedback from their managers — while individual underperformance is always possible, four consecutive exits from the same role over four months, all citing identical cultural concerns in exit interviews, is a pattern that makes individual performance failure an unlikely primary explanation; exit interview data showing consistent themes across multiple employees is precisely the signal that organizational and cultural factors are driving turnover
- **B:** ✅ **CORRECT** The expat managers' 'hands-on, collaborative' style reflects a low-power-distance, direct-communication management approach that reads as intrusive, disrespectful, and trust-denying in a higher-power-distance culture where role clarity and delegated authority are expected
- **C:** Hartwell's compensation structure is below market and the cultural complaints are secondary to a straightforward pay problem that exit interviews are not capturing — compensation analysis is always worth conducting, but exit interviews that consistently describe cultural and management style concerns — not pay — as the primary drivers are the most direct evidence available; attributing the exits to a hidden pay problem not captured in exit interviews requires dismissing the primary qualitative data source without any contradictory evidence
- **D:** The expats lack functional knowledge of the local market, and local employees are frustrated by being directed by managers who are less knowledgeable than the people they manage — functional knowledge gaps in expatriate managers are a legitimate management challenge, but the specific complaints described — feeling micromanaged, undervalued, and passed over for decisions — describe a management relationship problem rather than a competence credibility problem; employees frustrated by managerial ignorance typically complain about receiving bad strategic direction, not about being denied autonomy

**Correct:** B

### Q2

**Stem:** Hartwell's international operation is currently led entirely by managers recruited from Columbus headquarters and deployed internationally. Local hires fill sales, operations, and support roles but hold no senior management positions. This staffing approach is best characterized as:

- **A:** Geocentric — a geocentric staffing approach explicitly selects the best-qualified candidate for each role regardless of nationality, producing a mixed international management team; Hartwell's approach of deploying exclusively Columbus headquarters managers to lead international operations is the structural opposite of geocentric
- **B:** Polycentric — a polycentric approach places host-country nationals in local management positions because they understand the local market and culture; Hartwell's current model, which fills all senior positions with Columbus managers while local employees hold only non-management roles, is the direct opposite of polycentric
- **C:** ✅ **CORRECT** Ethnocentric — staffing international management roles primarily with home-country nationals from headquarters, reflecting a headquarters-knows-best assumption
- **D:** Regiocentric — a regiocentric model draws managers from within the geographic region of the operation — for example, staffing an Asian operation with managers from other Asian markets; Hartwell's approach of sending Columbus headquarters managers thousands of miles away does not fit the regional selection logic that defines regiocentric staffing

**Correct:** C

### Q3

**Stem:** Hartwell's Columbus HR team proposes implementing a US-style retention program built around individual performance bonuses — personal sales targets tied directly to quarterly cash payouts. A local HR consultant warns that this program may not achieve the desired motivational effect. What cultural principle most directly explains the consultant's concern?

- **A:** ✅ **CORRECT** In many collectivist cultures, individual financial rewards can actually undermine motivation because singling out individuals for recognition separates them from the group — violating a cultural norm where collective achievement and group harmony are the primary sources of professional meaning
- **B:** Pay-for-performance systems are prohibited by labor regulations in most emerging markets because they create compensation inequality that violates legal standards — this is factually incorrect; individual performance bonuses and pay-for-performance structures are legally permitted in the vast majority of emerging markets, and while specific plan designs must comply with local labor law on minimum wages and payment timing, a general legal prohibition on performance-based compensation does not exist in most markets where Hartwell operates
- **C:** Local employees are already earning above-market wages, so the marginal financial incentive will have little psychological impact regardless of program design — whether wages are above or below market is an empirical question that requires benchmarking data not provided here; even if wages were above market, motivation theory indicates that the structure and social meaning of rewards — not just absolute pay levels — determines motivational impact, particularly in collectivist cultures where individual financial rewards carry different social significance than group recognition
- **D:** High uncertainty avoidance in the local culture means employees strongly prefer stable base salaries over variable compensation, making any bonus program ineffective — high uncertainty avoidance does create a preference for predictable, stable compensation over variable pay, and this is a real design consideration; however, the deeper issue in collectivist cultures is the social disruption caused by singling out individuals for differential recognition, not merely the variability of the payment

### Q2

**Stem:** Hartwell's local distribution partner made a cash payment to a government official to expedite a stalled permit. Which statement best explains why the FCPA creates a problem for Hartwell — not only for the partner?

- **A:** The FCPA creates liability only for the individual who physically made the payment, not for companies that indirectly benefit from the payment — individual liability for the person who made the payment is one element of FCPA enforcement, but the statute explicitly extends liability to companies when payments are made on their behalf through agents, distributors, or joint venture partners; the FCPA's third-party liability provisions are among its most significant and most enforced features

- **C:** The FCPA applies only to corrupt payments made to national government ministers, not to low-level municipal officials who process routine administrative permits — the FCPA does not limit its coverage to senior officials; it applies to payments made to any 'foreign official,' a term defined broadly to include government employees at all levels, employees of state-owned enterprises, political party officials, and candidates for office, including municipal permit officers
- **D:** The FCPA applies only to US publicly traded companies reporting to the SEC, and Hartwell as a private company is not subject to its provisions — the FCPA applies to all US issuers and to all US domestic concerns, which includes privately held US companies, their officers, directors, employees, and agents; private company status creates no exemption from FCPA liability

**Stem:** Customs authorities have opened an 18-month audit of Hartwell's import documentation, citing inconsistencies in how products were classified under the tariff schedule. - **C:** Hartwell's distributor, as the local importer of record, bears all legal liability for customs classification, shielding Hartwell from the audit's consequences — while the importer of record bears primary customs compliance responsibility in many jurisdictions, this does not shield the beneficial importer or party directing the import activity from civil or criminal liability if systematic misclassification was directed by or known to the foreign supplier; customs authorities routinely pursue liability up the supply chain when intentional misclassification is found
- **D:** Customs misclassification is treated as a minor administrative matter that is routinely resolved through payment of the classification difference with no additional penalties — the minor-matter characterization applies only to isolated, unintentional errors discovered through voluntary disclosure; systematic misclassification resulting in material underpayment of duties is treated as a serious compliance violation subject to significant civil penalties, in some cases calculated as a multiple of the underpaid duties

- **D:** Local advisors have exclusive authority to negotiate directly with customs and regulatory agencies on behalf of foreign companies, making their engagement legally required before any audit response — companies facing regulatory investigations have the right to respond directly or through legal counsel; there is no exclusive right granted to local advisors and no legal requirement that foreign companies engage local intermediaries before submitting audit responses

### Q5

**Stem:** Following the FCPA incident, Hartwell's General Counsel recommends updating all distribution agreements to include explicit anti-corruption clauses — requiring the distributor to comply with the FCPA and comparable local anti-bribery laws, and granting Hartwell the right to audit and terminate if violations occur.
